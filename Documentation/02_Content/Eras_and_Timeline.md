# Eras and Timeline

## Calendar first, local adoption second

Eras can help organize art and content, but **years are the authoritative bounds for what can exist**. A city does not enter an era by buying a technology. Technology, vehicles, architecture, and fashions can arrive gradually, overlap, and remain long after newer forms appear. A prosperous connected city may adopt them earlier than a small remote town within the historically plausible window.

The discussion sketched a starting point around 1750, a nineteenth-century shift toward industrialization, a late nineteenth-century railroad and Gilded Age period, rapid innovation from roughly 1900 to 1945, and mid-century through contemporary development. These labels and boundaries are prompts for content planning, **not a settled era taxonomy**. Treat precise introductions—steam, canals, rail, automobiles, aviation, electricity, sanitation, architectural styles—as research tasks, sometimes at finer resolution than a broad era.

Historical presentation should avoid an abrupt global costume change on January 1. A 1790 building can survive into 2020. New styles may coexist with old streets and inherited infrastructure. This overlap is central to the visual and emotional promise of CityForge.

## Timeline content types

- **Availability windows:** when a technology, asset, institution, or service becomes plausible.
- **Historical context:** curated real-world events, regional conditions, and period language.
- **Local adoption conditions:** what lets a specific city use an available possibility.
- **Persistence and adaptation:** how older buildings and systems survive or change.
