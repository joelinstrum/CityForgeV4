# Infrastructure

## Service rather than maintenance

Water, sanitation, power, waste, and similar systems matter to city health and growth, but play should focus on **coverage, capacity, quality, and cost**. The player makes a meaningful investment when demand rises or conditions change, then returns to the city. Repetitive pipe drawing, truck routing, and routine repairs are outside the intended focus.

**Illustrative water progression:** an early low-density settlement beside usable fresh water may have water access by assumption. Houses away from it need wells. As density grows, wells become insufficient or water quality declines. A later waterworks can cover an area up to its capacity; improved technology extends or improves the service. Road connectivity might stand in for local distribution, but that detail requires prototyping. No exact ranges, dates, or capacities are decided.

Other services can follow the same design language where appropriate. A capacity warning is useful if it leads to a choice—expand, upgrade, add a facility, or rely on a regional provider. Some mature infrastructure may become assumed background service if manual intervention would add no interesting decision.

Infrastructure should leave visible marks and shape settlement. Waterworks, stations, roads, rail, canals, and ports give the city a physical story. Service quality can affect health, quality of life, costs, and development, with effects tuned for clarity rather than constant punishment.

## What CityForge is not

It is not a utility maintenance simulator. Lack of service has consequences; monitoring the same network every few minutes should not be the gameplay loop.

## Open questions

- Which services need player-placed facilities, and which can be assumed?
- How should overlapping coverage and capacity be explained in the UI?
- What events should trigger an alert instead of resolving in the background?
