# Time and History

## Two axes of progression

**Direction:** the year controls what is possible in the wider world; the city determines what it has adopted. A small town and a large industrial center in the same year may look and function differently. The town cannot leap ahead to modern vehicles in the 1840s by accumulating research points, but it also need not receive every current technology the moment the calendar allows it.

Availability can include architecture, vehicles, clothing, transport, infrastructure, institutions, and historical events. Adoption may depend on population, wealth, density, geography, trade access, policy, or demand. These are design factors, not settled formulas. Older forms can remain, overlap with new ones, or be adapted.

History is a **world context layer**, not a campaign script. The default American setting runs approximately 1750 to the present. Real events can affect the atmosphere, news, and occasionally relevant economic conditions, but they need not each become a gameplay mission.

## Levels of visibility

- **Ambient:** clothing, vehicles, construction styles, streets, industry, and landscape quietly change.
- **Surfaced:** a newspaper edition or small local event notice invites attention and can be ignored.
- **Deep:** players voluntarily inspect archives, buildings, neighborhoods, maps, and long-term statistics.

No score should depend on opening the historical UI. Consequences come from the world and player choices, not from clicking to read.

## What CityForge is not

It is not a technology race or a sequence of compulsory historical quests. The calendar should prevent obvious anachronisms, while development remains flexible enough for different regional stories.

## Open questions

- How quickly should game time pass, and can players tune it?
- Should history correlation or headline frequency be adjustable?
- Which historical events have simulation effects, and which remain context only?
